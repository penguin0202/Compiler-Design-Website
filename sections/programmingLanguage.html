Programming/Language

Grammar. Grammar is what's at play here. But I am getting ahead of myself ... or am I?

Let's actually examine the purpose of a programming language:

Quoting myself in the first section: "computers store and operate on things in ones and zeroes". Don't worry about the specifics of the specific ones and zeroes that computers need in order to do a specific thing, that'll be elaborated on later. For now, just know that in order for computers to do anything, it needs instructions in the form of ones and zeroes. (You could even say ones and zeroes are the language of the machine, iykyk)

The languages of humans on my other limb are much less rigid than ones and zeroes. Take the English langauge for example (get ready for a rapid-fire): New words are created with time. Definitions of words evolve gradually. Words with synonymous definitions have connotations that differently affect meaning. Meaning that can be emphasized though capitalizing or quoting. Meaning also sometimes derived from speaker tone. Or from the social environment. Or from the current cultural phenomenon. Or from the audience's emotions and feelings with certain words. Sometimes quoting words can be sarcastic, casting another shadow of doubt on whether things mean what they mean. And on doubting meaning, metaphors aren't to be taken literally. Most times, even with context, ambiguity persists. Perhaps the most used example is whether "she" in "Mary beat her daughter because she was drunk" referred to Mary or her daughter. Really, there just isn't a correct-ness to all of it. Language is fluid; with even its punctuations. More exclamations here for that little-bit more wow-ness; a huge trail of eclipsises here for that extra confusion. Really, in language, not just in the English language, there isn't a exact answer. Why do humans speak like this? I don't know I'm not a language expert. But what I can repeat is that human languages (dubbed "natural languages") are ambiguous, feeling-driven, and complicated.

So what's all this gotta do with programming languages? Well, we as humans need to find a way to communicate to computers our intentions, computers who only "speak" ones and zeroes. We don't speak ones and zeroes, and frankly, we don't want to. In fact, we speak "natural languages" that are far too messy and ambiguous for computers to understand. So how do we communicate with one another? If only there were a sort of intermediate language, a mediator, a bridge of some sort (woah a metaphor) that can connect human languages to machine language. Hmm...I wonder what that is...OH YEAH! It's a programming language! A programming language's purpose is for it to be easily understandable to humans (if you take the time to learn, that is), all the while maintaining a rigidity that can allow for it to be easily translated into ones and zeroes that computers can understand. If you're wondering why programming languages seem so distant from our natural languages, it's because they are designed to be a "language" people can be able to "program" computers in (the "language" part is very important later). A compiler, then, is to translate that programming language into ones and zeroes for the computer.
